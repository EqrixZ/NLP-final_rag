# Integrated Pest Management (IPM) Principles

## What IPM is
IPM is an ecosystem-based strategy that prevents pests and their damage over the long term by combining several techniques, while reducing risks to people, beneficial organisms and the environment. Pesticides are used only when monitoring shows a need, choosing the most selective, least hazardous option.

## Core components
### 1. Prevention
- Use healthy seed and transplants, resistant or tolerant varieties, and suitable planting dates.
- Practise crop rotation, sanitation (remove residues and weed hosts), balanced fertilisation and good water management.

### 2. Identification
Identify the pest or disorder correctly. Many insects are harmless or beneficial, and nutrient or weather problems can mimic disease.

### 3. Monitoring (scouting)
- Walk the field regularly, for example weekly. Check plants in a zig-zag or W pattern across the plot.
- Count pests and natural enemies, and note damage levels and crop stage.
- Use tools such as sticky traps, light traps and pheromone traps, and keep records.

### 4. Thresholds
- Economic injury level: the pest density at which the cost of damage equals the cost of control.
- Economic (action) threshold: a lower density at which control should start, so that the pest never reaches the injury level.
- Thresholds differ by crop, pest and growth stage; use locally published values.

## Control hierarchy
Use the least disruptive methods first and combine them:
- Cultural: rotation, sanitation, spacing, planting time, trap crops, pruning, irrigation management.
- Mechanical/physical: hand-picking, insect-proof nets, mulches, barriers, sticky traps, removing infected plants.
- Biological: conserve and encourage natural enemies (predators such as lady beetles, spiders and predatory bugs; parasitoid wasps) and use biopesticides such as Bacillus thuringiensis, Trichoderma, Beauveria and Metarhizium.
- Chemical: a last resort, based on monitoring. Choose selective products, spot-treat where possible, and follow the label.

## Natural enemies
Broad-spectrum insecticides often kill predators and parasitoids faster than they kill the pest. This can cause pest resurgence or secondary pest outbreaks. Avoiding unnecessary early-season sprays lets natural enemy populations build up.

## Resistance management
- Resistance is a heritable drop in pest sensitivity that leads to repeated control failure (IRAC definition). It develops when the same mode of action (MoA) is used again and again.
- IRAC (insecticides) and FRAC (fungicides) group active ingredients by mode of action using number or letter codes.
- Rotate between different MoA groups. Do not treat consecutive pest generations with the same group, and watch for cross-resistance within a group.
- Use pesticides only when thresholds are reached, at label rates. Combine them with non-chemical tactics to lower selection pressure.

## Farmer Field Schools in Asia and Thailand
The Farmer Field School (FFS) approach began in Southeast Asian rice fields under the FAO Inter-Country IPM Programme for Rice in Asia. Farmers meet weekly through a whole season. They use their own field as the classroom and carry out agro-ecosystem analysis: observing plants, pests, natural enemies and weather, then deciding together what to do. FFS is built on four principles:
1. Grow a healthy crop.
2. Conserve natural enemies.
3. Observe fields regularly.
4. Farmers become IPM experts.

Thailand has run rice and vegetable FFS through the Department of Agricultural Extension (DOAE). Community pest management centres continue this farmer-led approach.

## Practical note
Always read pesticide labels, use registered products only, and consult local DOAE or DOA officers for thresholds and approved methods for your crop.

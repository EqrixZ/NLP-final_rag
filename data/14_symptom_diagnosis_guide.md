# Plant Symptom Diagnosis Guide: From Symptom to Likely Cause

## Signs versus symptoms
- A symptom is the plant's reaction, such as yellowing, wilting, spots or holes.
- A sign is the organism itself or what it leaves behind: insects, mites, frass, webbing, fungal growth or bacterial ooze.
- Only living (biotic) causes produce signs. Abiotic disorders show symptoms with no signs.

## Biotic or abiotic? Look at the pattern
- Abiotic causes (nutrients, water, heat, chemical injury, poor drainage) usually affect a large area evenly. They often follow a sprayer pass, a low spot or a field edge, and may affect several plant species at once.
- Biotic causes (fungi, bacteria, viruses, insects) usually start in scattered patches, affect one crop species, and spread or get worse over time.
- Abiotic injury does not spread. Hail holes, for example, do not get bigger. Biotic damage keeps growing as the organism multiplies.

## Symptom → likely cause
### Wilting
- Sudden wilting of green plants with brown vascular tissue and milky ooze in the stem-in-water test → bacterial wilt (bacterium)
- Wilting with yellowing on one side and brown vascular streaks but no ooze → Fusarium or other vascular wilt (fungus)
- Wilting with dark, rotten roots or stem base → root or collar rot (fungus or water mould), often in waterlogged soil
- Whole field wilts at midday but recovers by evening → water stress or root damage (abiotic, or nematodes)

### Leaf spots and coatings
- Spots with rings or dark margins, sometimes with fungal growth → fungal leaf spot
- Water-soaked, angular spots limited by veins, sometimes with a yellow halo → bacterial leaf spot
- White or grey powdery coating on leaf surfaces → powdery mildew (fungus)

### Yellowing patterns (nutrient)
- Uniform yellowing of older, lower leaves first, with the whole plant pale → nitrogen deficiency
- Yellowing and browning (scorch) of older leaf tips and margins → potassium deficiency
- Yellowing between the veins of older leaves while veins stay green → magnesium deficiency
- Purplish older leaves and stunting → phosphorus deficiency
- Interveinal yellowing of young leaves → often iron or manganese deficiency, typical of high-pH soils
Mobile nutrients (N, P, K, Mg) show on older leaves first; immobile ones (Ca, Fe, B) show on new growth.

### Virus-like symptoms
- Mosaic or light and dark green mottling, distorted or puckered leaves, stunting → virus (often spread by aphids, whiteflies or thrips)
- Upward leaf curl with yellow margins in tomato or chilli → leaf curl virus (whitefly-transmitted). Similar curling can also come from thrips or mite feeding, or herbicide drift.

### Roots and galls
- Knotted swellings (galls) on roots → root-knot nematodes
- Brown, soft, sloughing roots → root rot (fungus or water mould) or waterlogging

### Insect damage
- Holes, ragged edges or skeletonised leaves, with frass → chewing insects (caterpillars, beetles)
- Sticky honeydew with black sooty mould → sap-sucking insects (aphids, whiteflies, mealybugs, scales)
- Rice plants yellowing then drying in circular patches ("hopperburn") → heavy brown planthopper infestation
- Silvery scarring or bronzing → thrips or mites

### Abiotic
- Scorched leaves after a spray, or twisting after nearby herbicide use → chemical injury or herbicide drift

## When to seek expert help
- When symptoms spread fast or the cause is unclear
- Collect fresh samples showing early and advanced symptoms (whole plant with roots if possible). Wrap in dry paper, keep cool, and photograph of the field pattern.
- Contact the local DOAE office, a community plant clinic, or a DOA research centre or university clinic. Soil or leaf tests confirm nutrient problems.
- Confirm the diagnosis before applying any pesticide, and follow the label.
